AI for Plant Operations: A Security-First Tool Gateway

Connecting AI assistants to plant ERP records, technical procedures, and device telemetry through a serverless AWS gateway built for least privilege, guarded writes, and full auditability.

By Danilo Díaz Tarascó

The problem on a plant

Running an industrial plant generates operational truth in separate places. The ERP holds sales orders, inventory, manufacturing orders, quality records, maintenance tickets, and equipment history. Standard operating procedures, troubleshooting notes, and wiring references live in a technical knowledge base. Live and historical device telemetry sits in its own monitoring stack.

Routine questions cross all three boundaries at once. An engineer or supervisor wants to know what happened to a specific machine, which maintenance tickets are open at a site, whether a field device is healthy, or what the documented procedure requires. Getting a reliable answer usually means switching portals, piecing together identifiers, or waiting on the one person who knows where a record lives. On the plant floor, the gap is wider because software has to work under touchscreens, gloves, and glare rather than a quiet desk.

Giving an AI assistant direct access to production systems is unacceptable without hard engineering boundaries. Shared credentials hide who acted, the gateway must enforce access, and an unguarded write against an ERP or equipment record can disrupt live operations.

The design

I originated, designed, and built a single serverless tool gateway on AWS using the Model Context Protocol, an open standard for structured assistant tooling. The gateway routes every tool call to one of three operational domains: the ERP, the technical knowledge base, and live and historical device telemetry.

People ask questions in plain language: what happened to this machine, which tickets are open at this site, whether a device is healthy, or what the procedure states. The assistant queries structured tools behind the gateway, grounds its answer in the actual records, and cites the source in business terms so the reader can verify it immediately.

The gateway sits inside a broader operational workflow designed for daily plant use:

  • Hybrid knowledge search: The team knowledge base combines keyword search with semantic search, so part numbers, error codes, and natural-language troubleshooting questions all resolve reliably.
  • Daily monitoring summaries: Automated operational summaries surface recurring issues and fleet health trends without requiring manual checks across every site.
  • Plant-floor interfaces: Companion interfaces are designed specifically for field conditions, prioritizing large touch targets, glove usability, and legibility under glare.

The security model

The gateway infrastructure enforces these controls for every tool call. Every control follows standard least-privilege practice for production enterprise and industrial systems:

  • Per-person identity: Every user signs in through single sign-on, and access tokens are strictly bound to the gateway as their audience so the token is intended for this service alone.
  • Deny-by-default permissions: An administrator manages explicit per-person, per-system grants. If a user is not authorized for a given backend or operation, the gateway rejects the call before any downstream request runs.
  • Read and write separation: Read tools and write tools are explicitly separated in the tool surface, preventing a routine lookup from crossing into a state-changing action.
  • Two-step guarded writes: Changing a record requires two distinct steps. First, the gateway prepares the exact proposed change for review. Second, the apply step executes only with a signed, expiring token and performs conflict detection to abort if the underlying record changed in the meantime.
  • User-attributed audit ledger: Every state change is written to an audit ledger under the authenticated person's identity, creating a clear operational trail.
  • Scoped service accounts and managed secrets: Downstream integrations run with minimum-rights service accounts. Credentials live in a managed secret store and never appear in prompts, tool outputs, or knowledge base documents.
  • Operational limits and observability: Response-size caps and execution-time limits bound the work each request can trigger, backed by health monitoring, alarms, and usage logs.

What it changed for the team

With one controlled entry point over operational records, operators, engineers, and managers spend less time hunting across disconnected interfaces and more time acting on verified facts. Questions about machine history, open tickets, device status, and documented procedures receive grounded answers with clear business sources instead of tribal knowledge or guesswork.

Just as important, separating reads from two-step guarded writes lets teams move past read-only demos into real operational workflows. People can prepare updates with confidence that concurrent changes are caught, permissions are enforced, and every action is attributable to the person who approved it.
